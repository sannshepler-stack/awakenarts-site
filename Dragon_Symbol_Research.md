# Dragon — Symbol Research

**Document type:** Phase A of the Figure Research and Development Standard (`AwakenArts_Figure_Research_and_Development_Standard.md`), per Susan's 2026-08-20 directive to suspend workshop production and conduct source-based research into the Figure, poem, imagery, symbolic relationships, language, mythology, literature, Scripture, and Jungian/archetypal scholarship before further workshop content is written.

**Method, per Susan's mid-research correction (2026-08-20):** This document does not reduce the Dragon to a concept that can be stated more easily than the image itself. It does not ask "what does the dragon mean" and answer with a proposition. It uses **amplification** — Jung's own term for circling an image with mythological, literary, linguistic, and cultural parallels, controlled and contextualized rather than a discursive chain running away from the symbol — to let the image's own resonance become intelligible without collapsing it into a single reading. Amplification requires "an extensive, even encyclopedic knowledge of myths, fairy tales, folktales, art, literature, and culture in order to specify precisely which parallels are archetypally pertinent" ([IAAP, "Amplification"](https://iaap.org/amplification-2/)). This document gathers those parallels. It does not resolve them into a facilitator's talking point, and it flags, rather than silently repeats, the places where AwakenArts' own existing material has already done what this method warns against.

That is the gap this document closes — not by discarding the catalog's intuitions, but by finding out where they hold up under amplification, where they need qualification, and where the poem's imagery is stranger and less settled than the catalog's own tidy phrasing suggests.

## I. The Poem Itself, Read Closely

*"The Dragon" (from* The Mermaid and Other Works*), Susan Ann Shepler ©*

The poem does not describe a hero confronting a dragon. It describes a dragon fighting itself, and it never once uses the word "self," "shadow," or "reconciliation." Read at the level of the actual lines rather than the catalog's summary of them:

> The dragon flung its tonnage tail, lined with half-plate razor scales, and struck the hissing furnace head with smoldering eyes of crimson red. The dragon flashed green and black, smashing, roaring, tearing back — and cranked its weight like an iron crane, with hellfire rage within its frame, torching breath across the sky to sear the dragon down one side. Dragon claws scored and scraped with dragon nails like metal rakes. With reckless aim, with cut and whack, dragon wings were slashed and cracked. The steaming dragon huffed and burned, with fireballs the dragon churned. The lightning battle tore the night with bolts of hate in the dragon fight.

Every verb of violence in this poem has the same grammatical subject and the same grammatical object: *dragon*. The tail strikes the head. The claws score the dragon. The wings — the dragon's own wings — are slashed and cracked by the dragon. This is not two characters in combat. It is not even, syntactically, one character fighting a part of itself in the way that phrase usually implies a self doing violence to an *other* part. The poem's grammar refuses the reader a second party to blame. There is only ever one noun doing and receiving the damage.

> Morning stepped with an eerie sound... [a] dragon lay dead on the battleground. A seething dragon fought alone in a dragon fight with only one dragon. The dragon didn't seem to know tails and heads are one somehow.

"A dragon fought alone in a dragon fight with only one dragon" is not efficient writing by accident — read amplifyingly rather than corrected as clumsy, it is the poem forcing "alone," "fight," and "one dragon" into direct collision three times in one sentence, as if the line itself cannot stop repeating the fact it is describing: singularity waging war against itself and calling it a fight. The poem's turn — "tails and heads are one somehow" — arrives not as insight but as loss. The recognition comes only after a dragon lies dead. This is a considerably darker sequence than "Not this or that, but and" (the poem's own closing motto, and the ampersand-as-revelation reading built on top of it in the catalog) prepares a reader to expect. The motto is arrived at by walking through an actual death, not around one.

> The shouting sun, the silent moon, the trembling baby stars — the battle was fought against its parts. The tail lashed upon the head, the head would see the tail dead.

*"The head would see the tail dead"* is worth sitting with rather than resolving. Grammatically ambiguous — does the head *want* to see the tail dead, or does the head simply, finally, *see* (recognize) that the tail is dead, too late? The poem does not disambiguate. Both readings are available and both are true to the image: the head's intent and the head's belated recognition are not different things in a creature fighting itself — wanting the end of the part you don't recognize as yourself, and only recognizing it as yourself once it's gone, may be the same motion described twice.

> Like two sides of a golden coin, the parts were made to live as one.

The closing image is a coin, not a body healed or a wound closed. A coin's two sides are never simultaneously visible to the one looking at it, and cannot be separated without destroying the coin's value as currency — a very different figure than "wholeness" or "reconciliation" as those words are used elsewhere in the catalog. A coin's sides were never fighting each other; they were never capable of doing so. That the dragon's two sides *could* fight, to the death, and a coin's cannot, is exactly the distance between what the dragon *was* doing and what it *was actually* the whole time — and the poem states that gap without explaining it.

## II. Amplification: Mythological and Literary Parallels

None of the following is offered as "the meaning" of the Dragon. Each is a resonance — a place where the human imagination has circled similar material before — held next to the poem, not substituted for it.

**The self-consuming/self-warring dragon vs. the guardian dragon.** The dominant Western dragon myth — Beowulf and the treasure-hoard, St. George, Perseus and Andromeda, Tolkien's Smaug — is Joseph Campbell's "guardian of the threshold": an external beast a hero must defeat or pass in order to claim a treasure, a princess, or passage to the next stage of the journey. Susan's poem does not use this structure at all. There is no hero, no treasure, no threshold to pass. The combat is entirely intransitive — the dragon has nothing to win. This matters for facilitation: language borrowed from the "hero defeats the dragon" tradition (conquest, victory, slaying) is available in the wider culture but is not this poem's own vocabulary, and reaching for it risks importing a structure the poem itself declines.

**The ouroboros.** The nearer mythological cousin is the ouroboros — the serpent or dragon that consumes its own tail, found in Egyptian funerary texts from roughly 1600 BCE onward and carried into Gnostic, Hermetic, and alchemical traditions ([Mythologis](https://mythologis.com/blogs/egyptian-mythology/ouroboros); [The Metaphysical Compass](https://themetaphysicalcompass.com/the-ouroboros-symbol/)). The ouroboros is usually read as a *resolved* image: tail and head already joined, a closed circle representing wholeness, eternal return, or — in alchemy specifically — "the unity of opposites: creation and destruction, beginning and end, matter and spirit" ([Noir Kāla](https://noirkala.com/blogs/kala-tales/the-ouroboros-wholeness-paradox-renewal)). Susan's dragon is the ouroboros *before* the circle closes — tail and head not joined but at war, the very thing the ouroboros already resolved shown here still unresolved, still lethal. The poem's closing line, "tails and heads are one somehow," is the exact claim the ouroboros makes visually and permanently. The poem arrives at the ouroboros's truth without ever showing the ouroboros's peace.

**Enantiodromia.** Heraclitus's term, adopted by Jung, for "the tendency of any one-sidedness to turn into its opposite" ([The Metaphysical Compass](https://themetaphysicalcompass.com/the-ouroboros-symbol/)) — is a genuine and precise parallel to a creature whose own rage, taken far enough, becomes the instrument of its own death. It is worth naming as a real resonance. It should not, per the amplification discipline above, be presented to participants as "what the poem means" — it is one more circle drawn around the same fire, not a translation of it into psychological vocabulary participants are expected to adopt.

**Jung on the dragon as shadow.** The existing catalog's "animus figure... action, will, conviction... rage in the shadow" language draws on real Jungian territory, not invented vocabulary. Jung's shadow is the disowned, unintegrated material of the psyche, and dragon/serpent imagery recurs across his own case material and his students' work as a face the shadow takes. Contemporary Jungian commentary states the parallel directly: "the dragon embodies the archetype of the 'shadow'... this battle with the dragon symbolizes the struggle with everything that has been split off and denied within ourselves," and further, "the dragon's split nature mirrors your own divided self — wisdom intertwined with darkness, protector fused with predator" ([This Jungian Life](https://thisjungianlife.com/dragon-the-archetypal-monster-and-ally-within/); [Mythical Archives](https://mythicalarchives.com/why-dragons-are-both-heroes-and-villains-in-myth/)). This confirms the catalog's instinct was not invented from nothing. It also sharpens it: Jung's shadow-dragon is usually a figure the *ego* (a separate character, a dreamer, a hero) must confront and integrate — subject meets object. Susan's poem, per the close reading above, gives no second party. The confronting and the confronted are grammatically identical. This is close to Jung's shadow work but not identical to its usual dramatic shape, and that difference is worth holding rather than smoothing over.

**Von Franz on the fairy-tale shadow.** Marie-Louise von Franz, Jung's closest collaborator, wrote extensively on how giants, monsters, and dragons in fairy tales personify "all the affects and attitudes that are unconscious to the ego personality" (*Shadow and Evil in Fairy Tales*) ([Shambhala](https://www.shambhala.com/shadow-and-evil-in-fairy-tales-1331.html)). Her method — reading a tale's images against a wide comparative field of other tales rather than assigning fixed one-to-one meanings — is itself a model for how this document, and eventually facilitation, should proceed. Direct access to her specific treatment of dragon-versus-dragon (rather than hero-versus-dragon) material was not available in this pass and is flagged as a genuine gap: if Susan wants this pursued further, the primary text (*Shadow and Evil in Fairy Tales*, *The Interpretation of Fairy Tales*) would need to be read directly rather than summarized secondhand.

**Augustine, again, from a different angle.** `Dragon_Christian_Tradition_Research.md` already identifies Augustine's "two wills... struggle within me... by their discord, undid my soul" as the closest tradition-language to the poem's own claim. Read next to the close reading above, the fit is even tighter than that document states: Augustine's insistence that the two wills are "both mine" — not a war between a good self and an invading enemy — is precisely what the poem's grammar enacts by giving tail and head no separate identity, no separate name, nothing but "dragon" throughout. This is independent confirmation, arrived at through a different method (close reading vs. patristic survey), of the same finding: the poem does not depict two things fighting. It depicts one thing not yet reconciled to itself.

**What the poem's imagery does not obviously connect to, and should not be forced toward:** the "family constellation" reading in the existing catalog (shouting sun / silent moon / trembling baby stars as "family systems, inherited patterns") is plausible but is the catalog's own extension, not something the poem's language requires. The poem calls the sun, moon, and stars witnesses to "the battle... fought against its parts" — cosmic scale given to an internal event, in the same register as Job or the Psalms giving creation itself a stake in one person's suffering, rather than a coded family-systems diagram. This is flagged, not corrected outright — Susan's own authorial intent for this passage has not been asked about in this research pass, and amplification should stop at "here is a resonance," not proceed to "and therefore this is what it means for your family."

## III. Where the Existing Catalog Holds Up, and Where It Doesn't

**Holds up, with real scholarly grounding now behind it:** the shadow/animus vocabulary; rage as "energy without understanding" rather than as an evil to be excised (this maps onto both the Jungian shadow-as-disowned-not-destroyed reading and the Christian Tradition document's independent finding that every tradition voice surveyed treats resolution as reconciliation, not the killing of one part); the refusal to end the story with the dragon slain by an external hero.

**Needs qualification:** "the Dragon functions primarily as an animus figure" states as settled fact what amplification suggests is one legitimate resonance among several (ouroboros, enantiodromia, Augustine's divided will) — worth keeping as a working frame, not worth presenting to participants, or continuing to state internally, as the single correct decoding.

**Needs the most caution:** the ampersand material. "The ampersand symbolizes realities that appear separate but belong together... it is not ornament, it is revelation" is the clearest instance in the existing catalog of exactly the move Susan's correction warns against — a rich, strange, violent poem compressed into a single typographic symbol standing in for its whole meaning. The ampersand is real, is in the poem's own closing line's spirit, and should not be discarded. But it is a conclusion, not an image, and building facilitation outward from the ampersand risks giving participants the destination before they have made the poem's actual, considerably darker journey through "the head would see the tail dead."
