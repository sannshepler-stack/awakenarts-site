# Dragon Figure Edition — Research: The Christian Tradition on the Dragon's Central Struggle

**Document type:** Prerequisite research, per Susan's directive "Research the Christian Understanding of the Dragon's Central Struggle," 2026-07-06. This document precedes and informs Scripture selection and page drafting for "The Larger Story" — it does not select a final passage or write the page. That decision follows this research, not the reverse.

**Method:** Began with the research questions, not with the dragon. Surveyed voices from the Christian tradition — pastors, spiritual writers, theologians, and classical sources — chosen because Dragon's specific struggle called for them, not from a fixed list reused for every edition: Joyce Meyer, Dallas Willard, C.S. Lewis, John Ortberg, Timothy Keller, Henri Nouwen, Brennan Manning, John Eldredge, Oswald Chambers, and A.W. Tozer, plus two classical voices (Augustine, Thomas à Kempis) whose influence sits underneath most of the above. Only after surveying how these voices describe the inner battle does this document return to Dragon and ask where the tradition has already recognized the same human reality, and where the edition should be revised.

**A note on scope, for future editions:** this list belongs to Dragon. Susan's direction is that every edition develops its own conversation with its own voices — Grismere might call for Lewis, Nouwen, Madeleine L'Engle, and Elisabeth Elliot; Bowls and Queen Ann will each surface different ones again, according to what their own symbolic movement actually needs. The point is not a standing roster of ten approved names; it's the discipline of asking the research questions before reaching for any name at all.

## Research Question 1 — How does Christian theology describe the inner battle?

Across every author surveyed, the inner battle is treated as real, near-universal, and not a sign of immaturity or failure — but the tradition is notably divided on *what kind* of battle it is, and that division matters for Dragon.

**One strand describes it as warfare against an intruder.** Joyce Meyer's *Battlefield of the Mind* frames the mind as contested ground where habitual wrong thinking ("strongholds") is built up over years and must be torn down through Scripture and the Spirit. John Eldredge's *Wild at Heart* uses explicitly martial language — "within each person, there is a civil war going on" — a false self built defensively around a core wound.

**A second, older strand — the one Augustine articulates most precisely — insists the battle is not between two separate natures or two selves, but within one will not yet unified.** In the *Confessions*, Augustine describes "two wills, one old, the other new... in conflict with me," then explicitly rejects the Manichean reading that would make this a war between a good self and an evil self: both contrary wills are *his own*. The self is not divided into two combatants; it is one self not yet gathered around its proper good. This is a meaningfully different claim than "there is an enemy inside you," and it is the reading Dragon's own text already makes: "not one thing fighting another... a single creature whose nature has simply not yet been reconciled within itself."

## Research Question 2 — What is the relationship between the mind, heart, will, and desires?

Dallas Willard's answer is the most systematic: transformation "must take account of the various aspects of our being: spirit (heart/will), mind (thought/feeling), body, social, and soul" — none of these can be addressed in isolation, and mature transformation reorganizes all of them around God rather than fixing one part (usually the mind, in popular treatment) while leaving the rest untouched. John Ortberg's *Soul Keeping* names the soul itself as the integrating center — "the life center of human beings," holding body, mind, and will together, and driving connection with God and others. Both push back against a common shorthand (fix your thoughts and the rest follows) in favor of a whole-person account much closer to what Dragon's own "one body, made of opposing parts" image already assumes.

## Research Question 3 — How is transformation described?

Consistently, as *received* rather than self-produced, and as *gradual* rather than instantaneous — two findings that matter directly for Dragon's own closing pages.

- **Received, not self-willed into being.** Oswald Chambers calls for "an absolute and irrevocable surrender of the will," not intellectual debate — and names a real crisis point ("the white funeral") that must be gone through, not reasoned around. A.W. Tozer: the way forward is "through the lonely valleys of soul-poverty and abandonment," relinquishing rival claims so God reigns "without a rival." Brennan Manning grounds this most starkly: identity comes *first*, as gift ("one beloved by God. This is the true self") — self-acceptance is "an act of faith in the grace of God alone," not a psychological technique.
- **Gradual, not instant.** Meyer: renewal happens "inch by inch, not instantly." Ortberg: transformation is "a continuous process," not a single fix.

## Research Question 4 — How does Scripture describe the struggle between the old and new self?

C.S. Lewis's reading of this in *Mere Christianity* supplies the clearest image, and it is unusually close to Dragon's own vocabulary: the change is "not like a fresh coat of paint, but a dyeing or staining which soaks right through," and what emerges afterward is not a uniform, generic self but *many distinct selves unified* — "in much the same way that the different organs of the human body are different from one another, and yet united in the facilitation of life." That is Paul's own image (1 Corinthians 12; Romans 12:4-5; Ephesians 4), and it is nearly identical in shape to Dragon's "one body, made of opposing parts that were never meant to be at war."

## Research Question 5 — How do respected teachers describe reconciliation, wholeness, surrender, sanctification, and renewal?

Henri Nouwen's contribution is the one most directly relevant to Dragon's specific plot, not just its themes. In *The Wounded Healer*, the wound is not something defeated and discarded — it becomes, once faced honestly rather than hidden, "a bridge to understanding others," even the substance of ministry to them. This is close enough to Dragon's own claim ("the dragon's rage... is the part of the dragon still waiting to be heard... often the thing asking, loudest, to be reconciled") that the two can be read alongside each other without strain. Timothy Keller's reading of the prodigal parable adds a different, complementary piece: reconciliation is not free of cost just because it looks effortless from the restored person's side — "somebody had to pay." Thomas à Kempis supplies the oldest and starkest formulation of the peace-through-surrender pattern: "there is no other way to life and true inner peace than the way of the Cross and of daily self-denial."

## Findings Across the Tradition: What Consistently Emerges

Five things surfaced across nearly every author, independent of tradition (Reformed, Catholic, Wesleyan-holiness, contemplative, evangelical-popular) and independent of era (4th century to present):

1. **The inner battle is not evidence that something has gone wrong with a person uniquely — it is treated as the common condition, named openly rather than hidden.**
2. **The most theologically careful voices (Augustine, and implicitly Lewis) resist reading the battle as two natures at war. It is one self not yet unified, not a house divided between a good tenant and a bad one.** This is the finding most directly relevant to Dragon, because it is the exact claim Dragon's own Recognition page already makes, arrived at independently.
3. **Resolution is consistently described as received, not self-willed.** Every author who addresses the *mechanism* of change (Chambers, Tozer, Manning, à Kempis, and Ortberg's "God's grace is the sole means of transcending the gap") lands on surrender and grace, not effort or insight alone.
4. **The wound or the struggling part is not merely tolerated once healed — in the strongest accounts (Nouwen especially), it becomes the very thing that equips a person to help someone else.** This maps directly onto Dragon's own structure: the dragon does not merely find peace privately; he becomes a messenger.
5. **Transformation is gradual and whole-person, not a single moment that fixes an isolated part (usually "the mind," in popular treatment) while leaving the rest untouched.**

## Editorial Objective 1 — Which biblical passages most naturally illuminate the Dragon story?

This research changes the shortlist from the previous pass, and confirms rather than overturns the direction taken there.

**Strongest candidate, and it survives this research better than it did the last review: the man restored from "Legion" (Mark 5:1–20; Luke 8:26–39).** It is a narrative, not a topical verse. Its shape matches Augustine's finding precisely — the man is not two beings at war (a "good man" fighting a "demon-man"); he is one person violently unintegrated ("Legion," many, not one), restored to being "one" ("sitting there, clothed and in his right mind"). The restoration is received, not self-achieved — he does not free himself. And, matching finding 4 above almost exactly, he becomes the messenger: sent to declare what happened to him. Every one of this research's five cross-author findings lands on this passage without needing to be argued into it.

**Supporting texts, not competing candidates:** Romans 7:14–25 (the divided will named directly — Augustine's own anchor text) could supply a single, brief, unquoted echo elsewhere in the edition (it already exists faintly in the Recognition page's language) without becoming the centerpiece, since it is argument, not story. 1 Corinthians 12 / Romans 12:4–5 (one body, many parts) is worth knowing as the text behind Lewis's organs image, in case a future edition's own figure calls for it more directly than Dragon's does.

**Genesis 32 (Jacob at Peniel), reconsidered:** still a real candidate, and worth recording as such rather than discarding — but it requires more explanation than this page's length allows (who is the wrestler; why does the blessing require a wound) and lacks the messenger beat. Ranked second, not disqualified.

**Correction, 2026-07-06 — the Legion narrative is rejected; Romans 7–8 is promoted from supporting text to centerpiece.** Susan reviewed the page built from the passage recommended above and rejected it outright: Dragon is not about demonic oppression, and Legion's narrative — however well its plot beats rhymed with Dragon's own (many-made-one, restored, becomes a messenger) — carries the wrong theological center of gravity. A passage can match a symbolic story's narrative shape while still misrepresenting what the story is actually about; shape alone is not sufficient. Dragon is about the divided human condition — false oppositions within one person (strength/tenderness, action/reflection, fire/breath) — and the need for their reconciliation under God, not deliverance from an external, invading force. Susan's governing test: not "which passage looks like the Dragon" but "what biblical truth makes 'and' possible."

Romans 7:14–25 — recorded above only as a supporting echo, set aside as "argument, not story" — is exactly right for this corrected premise, and Romans 8:1–2 supplies what the earlier pass didn't reach for: the resolution, not only the diagnosis. **Selected Scripture: Romans 7:15 (the divided will — "what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I") paired with Romans 8:1–2 (freedom in Christ — "no condemnation... the law of the Spirit of life... hath made me free").** Verified against KJV text via direct search. This also draws directly on Augustine's own reading (Research Question 1 above): the self is not two combatants, it is one will not yet unified around its true good — Romans 7 is Augustine's own anchor text for that finding, which makes its promotion to centerpiece a correction, not a departure, from this document's own research.

Susan's stated governing sentence for Dragon specifically, recorded here verbatim: "The Dragon is not about demonic oppression. It is about the divided human condition. The symbolic story awakens recognition; Scripture reveals why reconciliation is possible; Christ is the One who makes us whole."

**Second correction, same day — the Scripture-first question was still the wrong question; naming the central Gospel truth before Romans 7–8 exposes that the passage above repeats a milder version of the same error Legion made.** Susan's follow-up directive named the actual problem: Dragon's page had gone through six revisions because the underlying question kept changing, not because any single revision was badly executed. The corrected question is not "what Scripture fits the Dragon" but **"what aspect of the Gospel does the Dragon prepare the reader to receive?"** — asked and answered before any passage is named, per the method now recorded in `AwakenArts_Editorial_Philosophy.md` and `AwakenArts_Figure_Edition_Production_Standard.md`, Section 11.

*Naming the truth, independent of any passage.* Dragon's own recognition (established across every revision of Recognition and Living the Message) is this: parts of a person that feel opposed — strength and tenderness, action and reflection, fire and breath, self-protection and the longing for wholeness — are not actually enemies. They were mistaken for two warring halves and are, rightly seen, one whole. The very thing that was fought (the dragon's rage, his fire) becomes, once reconciled rather than defeated, the source of what he has to give. The aspect of the Gospel this prepares a reader to receive is not deliverance from an invading force (Legion) and not victory over a corrupting element that must be put to death (which is closer to what Romans 7–8 actually argues than the third correction above recognized) — it is **the reconciling, unifying work of Christ, in whom what seemed divided is made whole together, each part kept rather than defeated.**

*Testing Romans 7–8 against that truth, honestly.* Romans 7–8's own argument is real and was rightly identified as naming the divided will — but its resolution is bound up with mortifying the flesh: Romans 8:13, "if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live." That is a genuine Gospel truth, but it frames one side of the person (the flesh) as something to be put to death, not as a legitimate part to be reconciled and kept. Applied to Dragon, this would imply the dragon's fire and rage should be killed rather than reconciled — which is not what Dragon's own text says happens ("the very thing the dragon once fought... becomes, once reconciled, the wound that qualifies him"). This is the same category error the Legion narrative made, in a milder form: casting one part of the self as the thing to be defeated, when Dragon's actual claim is that both parts were always meant to live as one.

*A better fit, found by continuing to search rather than forcing Romans 7–8 to hold.* Colossians 3:9–11 names the truth precisely: the old self is put off, the new self is put on, "renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him," and the categories that once divided people dissolve — "Where there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcision nor uncircumcision, Barbarian, Scythian, bond nor free: but Christ is all, and in all." This is the biblical language closest to Dragon's own "not this or that, but and" — not a call to defeat one part, but a declaration that the old grounds for opposition no longer hold, because Christ is all of it, in all of it. Colossians 3:14–15 completes the movement with what holds the renewed self together — not force, but "charity, which is the bond of perfectness," and "the peace of God" ruling in a heart now called into "one body." **Selected Scripture, superseding the correction above: Colossians 3:10–11 (the new self, where former oppositions dissolve — "Christ is all, and in all") paired with Colossians 3:14–15 (love as the bond of perfectness; the peace of God ruling in one body).** Verified against KJV text via direct search.

This also resolves, more cleanly than the prior correction did, why the Lewis quotation ("not like a fresh coat of paint, but a dyeing or staining which soaks right through") was the right tradition voice all along: it names exactly what Colossians 3:10 says — total renewal, not surface repair — better than it ever fit Legion or Romans 7–8's flesh/Spirit contrast.

**Fourth correction, same day — Colossians rejected too: selected for matching vocabulary, not Dragon's actual movement.** Susan identified the pattern underneath this whole page's history repeating itself a third time: Colossians 3 was chosen because its words ("one body," "the bond of perfectness," "the peace of God") echoed the edition's own descriptive language, not because it told the same story Dragon tells. Restated plainly: **Dragon is not about unity between people. It is about division within the person.** Every correction on this page so far had been implicitly answering "what does Scripture say about unity" — the wrong question, however carefully the passage was chosen. The corrected question: *what does Scripture say about the divided heart?*

Susan supplied an expanded shortlist for this corrected question: Romans 7 (the divided will), James 1:8 (the double-minded man), Psalm 86:11 ("unite my heart to fear thy name"), Ezekiel 36:26 (a new heart), Romans 12:2 (renewing of the mind), Galatians 5 (flesh and Spirit). Romans 7 and Galatians 5 were set aside for the same reason Romans 7–8 was set aside in the second correction above — both resolve the divided condition through language of one side being put to death or overcome, not reconciled and kept. Ezekiel 36:26 and Romans 12:2 name transformation but not the divided condition itself as directly. **Selected: James 1:8 ("a double minded man is unstable in all his ways") paired with Psalm 86:11 ("unite my heart to fear thy name").** Both name the condition and its only remedy without casting either half of the person as an enemy to be defeated — Psalm 86:11 is a prayer *for* unity, not a claim that unity has been achieved by will or effort, consistent with the standing finding that resolution is received, not self-willed (Research Question 3, above). Both verified against KJV text via direct search.

**The Christian Tradition voice also changed, for a reason independent of the Scripture swap.** Henri Nouwen's quotation ("can make their own wounds available as a source of healing") was written to support the Returning-to-the-Dragon paragraph's own claim about the dragon's rage becoming a qualifying wound. Susan's direction to trim that paragraph — removing the retelling of the dragon's plot, including rage and fire — removes the very thing Nouwen's quotation was echoing. Replaced with Augustine's own description of the divided will, from *Confessions*, Book VIII (Pusey translation, 1838, public domain — no copyright clearance required, unlike Lewis's and Nouwen's estates): *"Thus did my two wills, one new, and the other old, one carnal, the other spiritual, struggle within me; and by their discord, undid my soul."* This was already identified in Research Question 1, above, as the single most precise account in the whole tradition of Dragon's own claim — one self not yet unified, not two combatants — and had been set aside twice before only because the page's Scripture kept pointing elsewhere. It is, on this fourth pass, finally the right fit for both halves of the page at once.

**The page itself is now substantially shorter**, per Susan's own instruction that restraint — not additional explanation — is one of Dragon's real strengths, and that a single confidently stated page outweighs three pages of over-explanation. "Returning to the Dragon" no longer retells the dragon's story (the reader already knows it); it moves directly from the figure to Scripture to the reader.

**Third note, same day — naming Dragon's own root question and the Christian answer, not a Scripture change.** Susan pressed one step further than "what aspect of the Gospel does the Dragon prepare the reader to receive" and asked Dragon's own question directly: *why do we fight the wrong battles?* Her answer: because the human heart is disordered by sin — but God has acted in Christ to reconcile us to Himself, to renew us, and to teach us to live in the truth. She named this as a richer foundation than "right versus wrong," because it explains *why* we so often mistake one for the other, and because it preserves Dragon as a story about every person rather than about villains or heroes — one of its strengths since the earliest drafts.

This names the diagnosis underneath the page's current text more precisely than the page states it. Colossians 3:9–11's own "old man / new man" language already carries this — the old self being put off is the sin-disordered self, not merely an unintegrated one — but neither the page's opening frame line nor its Scripture intro line names sin as the reason the false opposition arises in the first place; both currently describe the symptom (divided, opposed parts) without naming the cause. This is recorded here as an accurate answer to Dragon's own root question, worth holding alongside the page rather than folded into it immediately — consistent with Susan's own caution that this theological layer should not be revised at the same pace as layout or wording. Whether and how to work "disordered by sin" into the page's own language, without tipping the literary voice into doctrine stated outright, is left for a future, unhurried pass.

## Editorial Objective 2 — Which Christian themes consistently emerge across authors?

Listed in full above (Cross-Author Findings, five points). In one sentence: the battle is real, it is one self not two, resolution is received rather than won, the wound becomes useful rather than merely healed, and the whole process is gradual and whole-person.

## Editorial Objective 3 — Where has the Christian tradition already recognized the same human reality?

**A correction to how this question should be asked, and why it matters.** The first pass asked "where does the Dragon story already align with Christian understanding" — a question that quietly makes Augustine, Lewis, and Nouwen the judges of whether Dragon qualifies as sufficiently Christian. That gets the authority backward. Scripture is the authority; the voices from the Christian tradition surveyed here are witnesses, not validators. The right question is not whether Dragon passes a test these voices administer — it's whether Dragon's symbolic movement participates in a human reality Christians have already, independently, spent centuries contemplating. Augustine doesn't make Dragon Christian by agreeing with it. Augustine, Lewis, and Nouwen are evidence that the reality Dragon depicts is not a novel or private insight — it is one the tradition has long recognized.

Asked that way, more places resonate than the first pass gave credit for:

- "One body, made of opposing parts that were never meant to be at war" — the same human reality Augustine describes as one will not yet unified (not two natures at war), and the same reality Lewis pictures as organs of one body, distinct yet joined.
- "The dragon's rage... is the part... still waiting to be heard" — the same reality Nouwen names when a wound, faced rather than hidden, becomes able to serve someone else.
- "A wholeness like this is never self-made. It is received before it is lived" (added to Recognition in the first Christian Fulfillment pass; reworded 2026-07-06 from "peace" to "wholeness" per Susan's correction — the movement of Dragon's story is wholeness and reconciliation, and peace is only its result) — not just a literary turn. This is the one human reality nearly every voice surveyed here recognizes independently: that resolution comes by surrender and grace, not by insight or effort alone. That line should be kept regardless of what happens with the page's Scripture selection.
- "This encounter does not end when the page is closed... continues" (Living the Message) — the same reality the tradition names when it insists transformation is gradual, never a single fix.

These are documented as points of resonance to let inform revision, not as endorsements that authorize the edition. The literary voice of the edition does not change because Augustine or Lewis said something similar — it changes, if it changes at all, because the human reality itself asked for a truer word.

## Editorial Objective 4 — Where should the edition be revised so it faithfully reflects the Christian story?

One real risk, not yet a page-by-page defect: Dragon's language of "recognition" (the dragon *sees* rightly, and that seeing resolves the conflict) could, read in isolation and without the "received, not achieved" line doing its full work, drift toward implying that insight alone is what saves — a modern, therapeutic-sounding claim the tradition surveyed here consistently declines to make. This is not a reason to change Dragon's core vocabulary (recognition is the right word for what a reader experiences reading the edition) — it is a reason to make sure whatever page follows Recognition keeps naming that the wholeness recognized was *given*, not merely *seen*. The Larger Story, built around the Legion narrative, does this naturally: the man does not recognize his way to wholeness: Christ restores him, and recognition (the town seeing him "in his right mind") comes after, not before.
