## 1. Journey

### Symbolic Territory
Journey is one of Scripture's founding structures: a call to leave what is known, a period of not-yet-arrival, and a promise that only becomes visible after the leaving. Abraham's call in Genesis 12 is the root instance, but the pattern recurs throughout — Israel's Exodus and wilderness wandering, the Psalms of Ascent (120–134) sung by pilgrims walking up to Jerusalem, the road to Emmaus where two disciples recognize the risen Christ only in the walking (Luke 24:13–35), and the early church itself being called simply "the Way" (Acts 9:2, 19:9, 19:23, 24:14). Hebrews names Abraham and others "strangers and pilgrims on the earth" (11:13), treating pilgrim identity — belonging fully to no single place along the way — as a mark of faith, not a deficiency of it.

### Foundational Scripture
- **Genesis 12:1** — "Now the LORD had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee." The call precedes the map. The destination is not named at the moment of departure.
- **Hebrews 11:8** — "By faith Abraham, when he was called to go out into a place which he should after receive for an inheritance, obeyed; and he went out, not knowing whither he went." Scripture's own theological reading of Genesis 12 — naming the not-knowing as faith's shape, not faith's failure.

These two belong together: Genesis gives the narrative root, Hebrews gives Scripture's own interpretation of what that root means.

### Familiar-Language Field
"At a crossroads." "Finding my way." "Not sure where this road leads." "Taking the next step." "A new chapter." "One step at a time." "The road ahead." "Wandering" (used loosely, of a season without clear direction). "A leap of faith." "Setting out."

### Why It Matters to You
Journey gives dignity to forward motion that doesn't yet have a destination attached — a job change, a relocation, a long grief, a faith held before it's understood. It answers a real anxiety: the sense that one shouldn't move until the whole route is visible. Abraham's story says otherwise — that movement can be faithful, even devout, while the destination is still unnamed.

### Participant-Facing Copy (draft)
> **Journey**
>
> Abraham was told to go before he was told where. Genesis records the call plainly: leave your country, your people, your father's household, for a land I will show you (Genesis 12:1). The land isn't named. The route isn't given. What's given is a voice, and a promise that only comes after the leaving.
>
> Hebrews looks back on this and calls it faith: "By faith Abraham obeyed and went, even though he did not know where he was going" (Hebrews 11:8). Scripture doesn't treat this uncertainty as a flaw in the story. It treats it as the story's shape. Faith, here, isn't certainty about the destination — it's willingness to move before the destination is visible.
>
> We reach for this shape constantly without noticing. We say we're "at a crossroads," that we're "finding our way," that we don't know "where this road leads." Journey has become one of the ordinary languages we use when life won't stay still. Scripture got there first, and named something we still recognize: that movement can be faithful even before sight is given.

### Possible Connection to the Path of Awareness
Journey sits naturally early — it supports Recognition (that uncertainty and faithlessness aren't the same thing) and Awareness (that one is already in motion, already "on the way," before any destination is visible). A natural entry territory, without being fixed as a mandatory first stage.

### Recommendation
**KEEP.** The strongest-rooted of the four as it currently exists — a major, recurring biblical throughline, with an anchor (Hebrews 11:8) that already belongs to the symbol rather than being borrowed from elsewhere. Deepen rather than replace.

## 2. The Deep

### Symbolic Territory
"The deep" in Scripture is rarely a place — it's a condition: what is hidden, what is beneath the visible surface, what has to be drawn out rather than simply stated. The primordial deep opens Genesis itself ("darkness was upon the face of the deep," 1:2). The human heart is described the same way — not transparent, not fully known even to itself (Jeremiah 17:9). Wisdom is described as something hidden in the deep, not lying on the surface to be picked up (Job 28:12–14, "the deep saith, It is not in me"). Jonah descends into the deep as a place of near-annihilation and unexpected mercy (Jonah 2:5–6). Paul writes of "the deep things of God," searched only by the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:10). And the Psalms let the deep speak the register ordinary words can't reach — overwhelm, longing, being swept under (Psalm 42:7).

### Foundational Scripture
- **Proverbs 20:5** — "Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water; but a man of understanding will draw it out." Names the heart and deep water as the same image — the exact symbolic marriage this territory needs, and already this page's own scripture anchor (worth carrying forward).
- **Psalm 42:7** — "Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts: all thy waves and thy billows are gone over me." The felt register: not analysis of the deep, but the deep as lived experience — being overwhelmed by something too large to summarize.

Together: Proverbs names the deep as what must be drawn out; Psalm 42 names what it feels like to be under it.

### Familiar-Language Field
"Get to the bottom of it." "In over my head." "Deep down, I know." "Still waters run deep." "Digging deep." "Buried feelings." "Unspoken depths." "That hit deep." "Going deeper." "Drowning in it" (grief, work, feeling). "Plumbing the depths."

### Why It Matters to You
The Deep gives dignity to what's real but not yet available on request — motives we can't fully explain, grief that doesn't resolve on schedule, wisdom that can't be produced on demand. It reframes "I don't have words for it yet" as depth, not deficiency, and names the ordinary experience of being more than what's currently visible even to ourselves.

### Participant-Facing Copy (draft)
> **The Deep**
>
> Scripture doesn't treat the human heart as something simple. "The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters," Proverbs says, "but one who has insight draws them out" (Proverbs 20:5). What we carry inside isn't always available on request — it has to be drawn up, the way water is drawn from a well: slowly, often with help.
>
> The Psalms let the deep speak for itself: "Deep calls to deep... all your waves and breakers have swept over me" (Psalm 42:7). This isn't tidy language. It's what it feels like to be overwhelmed by something too large for a sentence — grief, longing, an ache with no clear edge. Scripture doesn't require that feeling to explain itself before it counts.
>
> We already talk this way. We say we're "in over our heads," that something hit us "deep down," that a person is "deep" when their inner life clearly runs further than what shows on the surface. The Deep names what we already sense: that we are not only what's visible, and that some of what's truest about us has to be drawn out rather than simply stated.

### Possible Connection to the Path of Awareness
The Deep supports Awareness and Integration — naming and honoring interior material that hasn't yet reached the surface. It's the territory most aligned with the "do not translate the symbol into a simpler proposition" principle established earlier this session; its entire content is about what resists premature naming.

### Recommendation
**REBUILD.** The underlying field is strong and genuinely distinct from Water (John 4:14 — thirst, renewal): The Deep is about hiddenness, the heart, and wisdom not yet drawn out. The existing scripture anchor (Proverbs 20:5) is sound and should carry forward, paired with Psalm 42:7. **The current coastal-path image does not visually support this symbol and should not be preserved on the strength of already existing** — flagged as a separate production/asset decision, not resolved here.

## 3. The Table

### Symbolic Territory
Few images run through Scripture as consistently as the table. Hospitality is treated as sacred obligation from the patriarchal narratives onward (Abraham's three visitors, Genesis 18). The Passover meal is itself the memory-making act (Exodus 12) — a meal that is also a re-enactment. Manna is daily table-provision in the wilderness (Exodus 16). David writes of a table prepared "in the presence of mine enemies" (Psalm 23:5) — provision that doesn't wait for safety. Jesus's table practice becomes one of the most contested features of his ministry: he ate with tax collectors and those the religious establishment considered sinners, and it scandalized onlookers (Luke 15:1–2) — table fellowship, in that world, was a public statement about who belonged. The Last Supper turns an ordinary shared meal into lasting covenant (Luke 22:19–20). On the Emmaus road, the risen Christ is recognized specifically "in the breaking of bread" (Luke 24:30–31) — recognition arrives at the table, not before it. And Revelation closes the whole arc with a table at the end of time: "the marriage supper of the Lamb" (19:9).

### Foundational Scripture
- **Psalm 23:5** — "Thou preparest a table before me in the presence of mine enemies." Provision offered in the middle of danger, not after it — the personal, pastoral register. (Note: this verse was present on the live `/encounters/table` page until a 2026-07-08 revision removed it, leaving only a non-scriptural Swindoll quote. Restoring it is not a new addition — it is a return to the page's own stronger prior material.)
- **Luke 22:19–20** — "This is my body which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me... this cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you." The table made into remembrance and covenant — the New Testament pole to Psalm 23's Old Testament one.

### Familiar-Language Field
"A seat at the table." "Come to the table." "Breaking bread." "Let's talk it over dinner." "There's always room at my table." "Open table." "She sets a good table" (hospitality). The word *companion* itself — from the Latin *com* ("with") + *panis* ("bread"): one who shares bread with another. This is a genuine, checkable etymology, not a stretch — the ordinary word for a close relationship already means "someone I'd share a meal with."

### Why It Matters to You
The Table names belonging as something enacted, not just felt — being given a place, being fed, being included at a table you didn't have to earn your way onto. It speaks to loneliness, to the difference between being tolerated and being welcomed, and to the vulnerability of receiving rather than achieving.

### Participant-Facing Copy (draft)
> **The Table**
>
> "You prepare a table before me," the psalmist writes, "in the presence of my enemies" (Psalm 23:5). Sit with that a moment — provision offered not after the danger has passed, but in the middle of it. The table doesn't wait for safety. It's set anyway.
>
> By the Gospels, the table has become one of the most contested spaces in Jesus's ministry — not because of what was served, but because of who was seated. He ate with tax collectors and the people the religious establishment considered sinners, and it scandalized the people watching (Luke 15:1–2). In that world, sharing a table was a public statement about who belonged. Jesus kept making that statement in favor of people who weren't supposed to be there.
>
> The night before his death, he took that same ordinary act — a shared meal — and gave it lasting weight: "This is my body... this cup is the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:19–20). The table became the place memory happens, the place a community keeps returning to in order to remember who it is.
>
> We still know, in our own language, that a table is never just furniture. We talk about "having a seat at the table," about who we'd want at ours and who we wouldn't, about "breaking bread" with someone as shorthand for real connection. Even the word *companion* comes from this — one who shares bread with another. The Table asks what it means to be welcomed somewhere your presence isn't earned. Only given.

### Possible Connection to the Path of Awareness
The Table speaks directly to Belonging and Connection — the relational, communal end of the Path. A natural counterweight to Journey and The Deep, which are more interior and individual; The Table is where the Path turns outward, toward other people.

### Recommendation
**REBUILD — strongly.** This is the richest field of the four, and the current page uses almost none of it: Psalm 23:5 was deliberately removed in a prior revision, leaving a non-scriptural quote as the page's only "Christian companion." This isn't a page that needs adaptation. It needs to be built again from what the symbol actually holds.

## 4. The Word

### Symbolic Territory
Genesis opens not with material but with speech: "And God said..." repeats ten times in the first chapter, and each time, what is spoken comes to be (1:3 and following). Creation is spoken into being before it is shaped by hand. John's Gospel deliberately echoes that opening line — "In the beginning" — and identifies the speaking itself as a person: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... All things were made through him" (John 1:1, 3), a Word who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (1:14). Hebrews calls the word of God "living, and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword" (4:12). The prophets consistently describe revelation arriving as address — "the word of the LORD came unto..." — not as private insight but as something spoken to them. Israel's central confession begins with an imperative to listen: "Hear, O Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4). And Paul roots faith itself in hearing: "faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17).

### Foundational Scripture
- **Genesis 1:3** — "And God said, Let there be light: and there was light." The Word's creative, world-making character, prior to any theological reflection on it.
- **John 1:1, 14** — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." John's Gospel is written to deliberately echo Genesis 1's opening — these two passages are not independently chosen parallels; John is intentionally reading his own opening against Genesis's.

This pairing is the strongest available foundation for The Word — entirely distinct from Lamp's Psalm 119:105, which stays with Lamp.

### Familiar-Language Field
"Your word is your bond." "I'll take you at your word." "Actions speak louder than words." "Words have power." "She has a way with words." "Words that stuck with me." "I need to hear it" (not just know it). "Spoke it into existence" — a genuinely common contemporary idiom that, whether or not the speaker intends it, echoes Genesis 1's own logic more closely than almost any other phrase in ordinary use.

### Why It Matters to You
The Word names the weight of speech — that words aren't neutral sound but carry creative and relational force. It speaks to the difference between being seen and being heard, to the power of a promise spoken aloud, an apology given, a blessing said directly to someone rather than only felt. It also gives theological ground to a method this whole project already uses: that ordinary language already carries symbolic weight, worth noticing rather than passing over.

### Participant-Facing Copy (draft)
> **The Word**
>
> Genesis opens with speech, not material. "And God said, Let there be light: and there was light" (Genesis 1:3). Nine more times in that first chapter, the same pattern holds — God speaks, and what is spoken comes to be. In this telling, creation isn't shaped by hand before it's shaped by word. The word comes first.
>
> John's Gospel picks up that same opening line — "In the beginning" — and takes it further: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... All things were made through him" (John 1:1, 3). Then further still: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). The same creative speech that spoke light into being is, in John's account, someone you could stand next to.
>
> We already trust that words carry more than information. We say someone's "word is their bond." We say words can build up or tear down. People now say they "spoke something into existence" — language that, without necessarily meaning to, echoes Genesis far more closely than most idioms do. The Word asks us to take seriously what we already sense: that speech isn't incidental to reality, and that being spoken to — really heard, really addressed — is one of the oldest ways human beings have of knowing they are not alone.

### Possible Connection to the Path of Awareness
The Word supports Recognition and Integration most directly — recognizing that language itself shapes reality and identity. It's also the one symbol that makes AwakenArts's own working method explicit as theology: the site already treats ordinary metaphor as a place symbolic meaning lives (the homepage's "Path of Stones" section, the Familiar-Language Field standard). The Word is where that method's own premise is named outright.

### Recommendation
**REBUILD.** Needs entirely new grounding — Genesis 1 and John 1, not Psalm 119:105, which remains Lamp's verse. Once properly resourced, this is a strong and clearly distinct field; as it stands today it's underdeveloped, borrowing rather than standing on its own ground.
